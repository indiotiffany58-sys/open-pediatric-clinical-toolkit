## Section 1: Neurology & Seizure Disorders

### Question 1: Acute Convulsive Status Epilepticus
**Vignette:** A 2-year-old boy weighing 12 kg is brought to the emergency department actively having a generalized tonic-clonic seizure that started 8 minutes ago at home. Temperature is 39.2°C, heart rate is 160 bpm, and oxygen saturation is 96% on blow-by oxygen. Point-of-care blood glucose is 94 mg/dL. IV access is established.  
**Question:** Which of the following is the most appropriate immediate pharmacological intervention?
- A) Phenytoin 240 mg IV infused over 20 minutes
- B) Lorazepam 1.2 mg IV slow push
- C) Levetiracetam 120 mg IV bolus
- D) Phenobarbital 240 mg IM
- E) Propofol 24 mg IV push

**Correct Answer:** B  
**Rationale:** First-line therapy for convulsive status epilepticus (>5 minutes) is a rapid-acting benzodiazepine. IV lorazepam is dosed at 0.1 mg/kg (1.2 mg for a 12 kg child), up to a maximum of 4 mg per dose.

### Question 2: Simple vs. Complex Febrile Seizures
**Vignette:** An 18-month-old girl presents after a 3-minute generalized tonic-clonic seizure associated with a fever of 38.9°C secondary to acute otitis media. Fifteen minutes later, she is awake, playful, and has a normal neurological examination. She has no prior history of seizures and her immunizations are up to date.  
**Question:** What is the most appropriate next step in the diagnostic evaluation of this patient?
- A) Emergent non-contrast head CT
- B) Outpatient routine electroencephalogram (EEG)
- C) Lumbar puncture for CSF analysis
- D) Reassurance, antipyretics, and treatment of the underlying otitis media without neurodiagnostic testing
- E) Initiation of daily oral levetiracetam prophylaxis

**Correct Answer:** D  
**Rationale:** A generalized seizure lasting less than 15 minutes that does not recur within 24 hours in a neurologically healthy child aged 6–60 months is a simple febrile seizure. No neuroimaging, EEG, or lumbar puncture is indicated if the child returns to baseline and lacks meningeal signs.

### Question 3: Second-Line Management of Refractory Status Epilepticus
**Vignette:** A 4-year-old boy (weight: 16 kg) continues to exhibit generalized convulsive activity after receiving two adecuadamente spaced doses of IV lorazepam (0.1 mg/kg each). Airway remains patent with supplemental oxygen.  
**Question:** Which of the following is the most appropriate second-line antiepileptic regimen?
- A) Midazolam 0.05 mg/kg PO
- B) Levetiracetam 960 mg (60 mg/kg) IV infused over 10–15 minutes
- C) Carbamazepine 320 mg via nasogastric tube
- D) Ethosuximide 500 mg IV push
- E) Topiramate 100 mg PO

**Correct Answer:** B  
**Rationale:** According to ESETT trial data and status epilepticus guidelines, levetiracetam (60 mg/kg, max 4500 mg), fosphenytoin (20 mg PE/kg), or valproate (40 mg/kg) are equally effective second-line agents for benzodiazepine-refractory status epilepticus.

### Question 4: Childhood Absence Epilepsy
**Vignette:** A 7-year-old girl is evaluated for frequent episodes of "daydreaming" noticed by her teacher. During the clinic visit, hyperventilation for 2 minutes triggers a 10-second episode of abrupt behavioral arrest and eyelid fluttering, followed by immediate return to baseline without postictal confusion.  
**Question:** What is the expected finding on an EEG, and what is the first-line treatment?
- A) Hypsarrhythmia; ACTH
- B) Centrotemporal spikes; Oxcarbazepine
- C) Generalized 3-Hz spike-and-wave discharges; Ethosuximide
- D) Slow <2.5-Hz spike-and-wave complexes; Rufinamide
- E) Generalized polyspikes; Phenytoin

**Correct Answer:** C  
**Rationale:** Childhood absence epilepsy is characterized by brief staring spells without postictal confusion, classically provoked by hyperventilation. EEG shows 3-Hz generalized spike-and-wave discharges, and ethosuximide is the drug of choice.

### Question 5: Infantile Spasms (West Syndrome)
**Vignette:** A 6-month-old infant presents with clusters of sudden, symmetric flexion of the neck, trunk, and extremities occurring primarily upon awakening. The parents also note that the infant has stopped rolling over and no longer tracks faces. Wood lamp examination reveals hypopigmented macules shaped like ash leaves.  
**Question:** Which medication is specifically first-line for infantiles spasms associated with this underlying neurocutaneous syndrome?
- A) Vigabatrin
- B) Gabapentin
- C) Lamotrigine
- D) Carbamazepine
- E) Phenytoin

**Correct Answer:** A  
**Rationale:** The patient has Infantile Spasms secondary to Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (ash-leaf spots). While ACTH or high-dose prednisolone are first-line for most etiologies of West syndrome, vigabatrin (a GABA transaminase inhibitor) is the treatment of choice when caused by Tuberous Sclerosis.

## Section 2: Hematology & Oncology

### Question 6: Nutritional Iron Deficiency Anemia
**Vignette:** A 15-month-old toddler is brought for a well-child check. He drinks 36 oz (1,080 mL) of whole cow's milk daily and is a picky eater. Labs show Hemoglobin 8.4 g/dL, MCV 62 fL, RDW 18.5%, and reticulocyte count 0.8%.  
**Question:** In addition to limiting cow's milk intake to less than 16–20 oz/day, what is the recommended therapeutic oral elemental iron dose?
- A) 0.5 mg/kg/day
- B) 1 mg/kg/day
- C) 3–6 mg/kg/day of elemental iron
- D) 15–20 mg/kg/day of elemental iron
- E) Parenteral iron dextran infusion immediately

**Correct Answer:** C  
**Rationale:** Uncomplicated nutritional iron deficiency anemia in toddlers is treated with oral elemental iron at 3 to 6 mg/kg/day alongside dietary modification (reducing cow's milk and increasing iron-rich and vitamin C-containing foods).

### Question 7: Differentiation of Microcytic Anemia
**Vignette:** A 3-year-old boy of Mediterranean descent is evaluated for mild microcytic anemia. Labs reveal Hemoglobin 10.8 g/dL, MCV 64 fL, RBC count 5.8 × 10^6/µL, RDW 12.2%, and normal serum ferritin.  
**Question:** Calculation of the Mentzer index (MCV/RBC) yields 11.0. What is the most likely diagnosis?
- A) Iron deficiency anemia
- B) Lead poisoning
- C) Beta-thalassemia trait
- D) Anemia of chronic disease
- E) Sideroblastic anemia

**Correct Answer:** C  
**Rationale:** A Mentzer index (MCV divided by RBC count in millions) of less than 13 coupled with a normal RDW and normal ferritin strongly points toward thalassemia trait rather than iron deficiency anemia (where the Mentzer index is typically >13 and RDW is elevated).

### Question 8: Expected Reticulocyte Response to Iron Therapy
**Vignette:** A 14-month-old girl with severe nutritional iron deficiency anemia (initial Hb 7.2 g/dL) is started on oral ferrous sulfate (4 mg/kg/day of elemental iron). The parents confirm strict adherence to the medication.  
**Question:** When does peak reticulocytosis typically occur after initiating oral iron replacement?
- A) Within 6–12 hours
- B) At 5 to 10 days
- C) At 4 to 6 weeks
- D) At 3 to 6 months
- E) Only after ferritin exceeds 100 ng/mL

**Correct Answer:** B  
**Rationale:** Reticulocytosis begins within 72 hours and peaks between 5 and 10 days after initiating oral iron therapy, followed by a rise in hemoglobin of approximately 1 g/dL every 2–3 weeks. Replenishing bone marrow iron stores (ferritin) takes 3 months.

### Question 9: Immune Thrombocytopenic Purpura (ITP)
**Vignette:** A previously healthy 4-year-old girl presents with sudden onset of generalized petechiae and bruising 2 weeks after recovering from a viral upper respiratory infection. She has no mucosal bleeding, hepatosplenomegaly, or lymphadenopathy. CBC shows WBC 7,200/µL, Hb 12.4 g/dL, and platelets 14,000/µL. Peripheral smear shows large platelets and normal leukocyte morphology.  
**Question:** What is the most appropriate management for this patient?
- A) Immediate platelet transfusion
- B) Bone marrow aspiration and biopsy
- C) Emergency splenectomy
- D) Observation and activity restriction (avoiding contact sports and NSAIDs)
- E) High-dose cyclophosphamide

**Correct Answer:** D  
**Rationale:** In children with newly diagnosed ITP who have cutaneous symptoms only (petechiae/purpura) and no mucosal bleeding, observation alone is recommended regardless of the platelet count. IVIG or corticosteroids are reserved for mucosal bleeding or high-risk situations.

### Question 10: Acute Chest Syndrome in Sickle Cell Disease
**Vignette:** An 8-year-old boy with HbSS disease hospitalized for a vaso-occlusive pain crisis develops fever (38.8°C), tachypnea, chest pain, and an oxygen saturation of 88% on room air. Chest radiograph reveals a new segmental pulmonary infiltrate in the right lower lobe.  
**Question:** Which combination of therapies is indicated for this life-threatening complication?
- A) High-dose loop diuretics and fluid restriction
- B) Oxygen, empirical IV cephalosporin + macrolide, incentive spirometry, and RBC transfusion
- C) Inhaled racemic epinephrine and oral dexamethasone
- D) Immediate thoracentesis and chest tube placement
- E) High-dose aspirin and heparin infusion

**Correct Answer:** B  
**Rationale:** Acute Chest Syndrome (defined by a new pulmonary infiltrate on CXR plus fever or respiratory symptoms in a patient with SCD) requires oxygen support, pain control, incentive spirometry, broad-spectrum antibiotics covering typical and atypical pathogens (ceftriaxone + azithromycin), and simple or exchange transfusion.

## Section 3: Shock, Resuscitation & Emergency Care

### Question 11: Compensated vs. Hypotensive Septic Shock
**Vignette:** A 3-year-old girl presents with fever, lethargy, and poor oral intake. Examination shows HR 175 bpm, RR 42/min, BP 88/54 mmHg, mottled skin, cool extremities, weak peripheral pulses, and capillary refill time of 5 seconds.  
**Question:** How should this patient's hemodynamic state be classified?
- A) Normal febrile response
- B) Compensated distributive/cold shock
- C) Decompensated hypotensive shock
- D) Neurogenic shock
- E) Obstructive shock

**Correct Answer:** B  
**Rationale:** In pediatrics, hypotension is a late sign of shock (for a 3-year-old, systolic hypotension is defined as <70 + [2 × age in years] = <76 mmHg). Tachycardia, delayed capillary refill (>2 sec), and cool mottled extremities with a preserved systolic BP indicate compensated shock.

### Question 12: Initial Fluid Resuscitation in Pediatric Shock
**Vignette:** A 10-kg infant arrives in the emergency department in hypovolemic shock secondary to severe acute gastroenteritis. Intraosseous access is rapidly secured in the proximal tibia.  
**Question:** What is the standard initial crystalloid bolus volume and rate of administration?
- A) 5 mL/kg of D5W over 60 minutes
- B) 20 mL/kg (200 mL) of 0.9% Normal Saline or Lactated Ringer's over 5–20 minutes
- C) 50 mL/kg of 0.45% Saline over 10 minutes
- D) 100 mL/kg of 3% Hypertonic Saline push
- E) Maintenance fluids only using the Holliday-Segar formula

**Correct Answer:** B  
**Rationale:** Pediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS) guidelines recommend an initial isotonic crystalloid bolus (0.9% NS or LR) of 20 mL/kg administered over 5 to 20 minutes for hypovolemic and septic shock, with frequent reassessment after each bolus.

### Question 13: Pediatric Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (Two-Rescuer Ratio)
**Vignette:** A 5-year-old boy suffers a witnessed collapse in the emergency department and is found to be unresponsive, apneic, and pulseless. Two healthcare providers immediately initiate high-quality CPR while the defibrillator is being attached.  
**Question:** What is the correct compression-to-ventilation ratio and compression depth for two-rescuer pediatric CPR?
- A) 30:2 ratio; depth of 2 cm
- B) 15:2 ratio; depth of approximately one-third the anteroposterior diameter of the chest (~5 cm)
- C) 15:1 ratio; depth of 3 cm
- D) 3:1 ratio; depth of one-half the chest diameter
- E) Continuous compressions without pauses before intubation

**Correct Answer:** B  
**Rationale:** For infants and children prior to puberty, two-rescuer CPR uses a 15:2 compression-to-ventilation ratio (whereas single-rescuer CPR uses 30:2). Compression depth should be at least one-third of the AP chest diameter (about 4 cm in infants and 5 cm in children).

### Question 14: Defibrillation and Epinephrine Dosing in Pediatric Cardiac Arrest
**Vignette:** A 20-kg child is in pulseless ventricular fibrillation. After 2 minutes of CPR and an initial shock of 40 J (2 J/kg), the rhythm check shows persistent ventricular fibrillation.  
**Question:** What is the appropriate energy dose for the second shock, and what is the correct IV/IO epinephrine dose?
- A) 20 J (1 J/kg) shock; Epinephrine 1 mg (1:1,000) IV
- B) 80 J (4 J/kg) shock; Epinephrine 0.2 mg (0.01 mg/kg of 0.1 mg/mL concentration) IV/IO
- C) 200 J shock; Epinephrine 2 mg IV
- D) Synchronized cardioversion at 10 J; Atropine 0.4 mg IV
- E) No further shocks; Amiodarone 300 mg IV push

**Correct Answer:** B  
**Rationale:** PALS dosing for defibrillation is 2 J/kg for the first shock, 4 J/kg for the second shock, and ≥4 J/kg (up to 10 J/kg or adult dose) for subsequent shocks. Epinephrine in cardiac arrest is dosed at 0.01 mg/kg (0.1 mL/kg of the 0.1 mg/mL [1:10,000] concentration), which equals 0.2 mg for a 20-kg child.

### Question 15: Pediatric Anaphylaxis Management
**Vignette:** A 6-year-old boy (weight: 22 kg) develops diffuse urticaria, stridor, wheezing, and hypotension (BP 68/40 mmHg) 10 minutes after eating a peanut-containing snack.  
**Question:** What is the exact dose, concentration, and route of the life-saving first-line medication?
- A) Diphenhydramine 25 mg IV push
- B) Methylprednisolone 44 mg IV push
- C) Epinephrine 0.22 mg (0.01 mg/kg of 1 mg/mL concentration) IM into the mid-anterolateral thigh
- D) Epinephrine 2.2 mg IV bolus
- E) Albuterol nebulization alone

**Correct Answer:** C  
**Rationale:** Intramuscular epinephrine (1 mg/mL or 1:1,000 concentration) injected into the vastus lateralis (mid-anterolateral thigh) at 0.01 mg/kg (max 0.3 mg in prepubertal children, 0.5 mg in adolescents) is the primary treatment for anaphylaxis.

## Section 4: Nephrology & Urinary Tract Disorders

### Question 16: Urine Collection in Non-Toilet-Trained Febrile Infants
**Vignette:** An 8-month-old uncircumcised male infant presents with a 2-day history of fever up to 39.5°C without an obvious source on physical examination. A urinary tract infection is suspected, and empirical antibiotic therapy is planned.  
**Question:** Which method is required to obtain a definitive urine specimen for culture prior to starting antibiotics?
- A) Perineal adhesive urine collection bag for culture
- B) Transurethral bladder catheterization or suprapubic aspiration
- C) Squeezing urine from the diaper
- D) Treat empirically without obtaining a urine culture
- E) 24-hour timed urine collection

**Correct Answer:** B  
**Rationale:** Per AAP guidelines, a bagged urine specimen has an unacceptably high false-positive rate (up to 85%) due to periurethral contamination and can only be used to rule out UTI if urinalysis is completely negative. A specimen via catheterization or suprapubic aspiration is mandatory before antibiotics in non-toilet-trained children.

### Question 17: Imaging After a First Febrile UTI
**Vignette:** A 10-month-old girl responds well to 10 days of cephalexin for her first culture-confirmed *Escherichia coli* febrile urinary tract infection. She becomes afebrile within 24 hours of initiating treatment.  
**Question:** What imaging study is routinely recommended following a first febrile UTI in an infant between 2 and 24 months of age?
- A) Renal and bladder ultrasonography (RBUS) only
- B) Immediate voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) in all patients
- C) Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT scan
- D) Intravenous pyelogram (IVP)
- E) No imaging is indicated until the third UTI

**Correct Answer:** A  
**Rationale:** AAP guidelines recommend a renal and bladder ultrasound (RBUS) after a first febrile UTI in children aged 2–24 months to detect anatomic abnormalities or hydronephrosis. VCUG is no longer routine after a first UTI unless the RBUS shows hydronephrosis, scarring, or findings suggestive of high-grade VUR, or if the febrile UTI recurs.

### Question 18: Minimal Change Disease (Nephrotic Syndrome)
**Vignette:** A 3-year-old boy presents with progressive periorbital edema that is worse in the mornings and bilateral pitting pedal edema. Blood pressure is normal. Urinalysis shows 4+ protein and no RBCs. Spot urine protein-to-creatinine ratio is 4.5, serum albumin is 1.8 g/dL, and total cholesterol is 340 mg/dL. Serum C3 and C4 levels are normal.  
**Question:** What is the most appropriate next step in management?
- A) Urgent percutaneous renal biopsy prior to treatment
- B) Empirical oral prednisone therapy
- C) High-dose IV cyclophosphamide
- D) Bilateral nephrectomy
- E) Lifetime ACE inhibitor monotherapy

**Correct Answer:** B  
**Rationale:** In children aged 1–10 years presenting with classic features of idiopathic nephrotic syndrome (edema, heavy proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, normal complement, and absence of hypertension or gross hematuria), Minimal Change Disease accounts for >85% of cases. Empirical corticosteroid therapy (prednisone) is initiated without a renal biopsy.

### Question 19: Post-Streptococcal Glomerulonephritis (PSGN)
**Vignette:** A 7-year-old boy presents with cola-colored urine, periorbital puffiness, and a blood pressure of 128/84 mmHg. Two weeks ago, he was treated for impetigo. Urinalysis reveals dysmorphic RBCs, RBC casts, and 2+ protein.  
**Question:** Which complement profile is characteristic of this condition?
- A) Normal C3 and Normal C4
- B) Low C3 and Normal C4, normalizing within 6–8 weeks
- C) Low C3 and Low C4 persisting beyond 6 months
- D) Isolated C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency
- E) Elevated C3 and Elevated CH50

**Correct Answer:** B  
**Rationale:** Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis activates the alternative complement pathway, resulting in depressed C3 levels with typically normal C4 levels. C3 levels return to normal within 6 to 8 weeks; persistence of low C3 beyond 8 weeks warrants renal biopsy to rule out C3 glomerulopathy or lupus nephritis.

### Question 20: Typical Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (HUS)
**Vignette:** A 4-year-old girl develops pallor, petechiae, and oliguria 5 days after an episode of bloody diarrhea following a visit to a petting zoo. Labs reveal Hb 6.8 g/dL, platelets 32,000/µL, creatinine 2.4 mg/dL, elevated LDH, and abundant schistocytes on peripheral blood smear.  
**Question:** Which intervention is contraindicated in the management of Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC) HUS?
- A) Careful fluid and electrolyte management
- B) Packed red blood cell transfusion for symptomatic severe anemia
- C) Systemic antibiotics and antimotility agents
- D) Renal replacement therapy (dialysis) if uremia or refractory hyperkalemia develops
- E) Blood pressure control for hypertension

**Correct Answer:** C  
**Rationale:** Antibiotics and antimotility agents are contraindicated in STEC-associated bloody diarrhea/HUS because bacterial lysis increases the release and intestinal absorption of Shiga toxin (Stx1/Stx2), worsening endothelial damage and microangiopathic progression.
